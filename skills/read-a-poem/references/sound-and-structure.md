# Sound and structure terms

Use a term only when it names something you can point to in the text. A reading is not better for having more terms in it.

## Sound devices

| Term | What it is | Check before claiming it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alliteration | The same consonant sound at the start of nearby stressed syllables | It is about sound, not letters: "knight" and "night" alliterate, "cat" and "city" do not |
| Assonance | The same vowel sound in nearby stressed syllables with different consonants after | Compare vowel symbols from `pronounce_word` when unsure. Spelling misleads |
| Consonance | The same consonant sounds in nearby words, most clearly at the ends, with different vowels | Final consonants should match in sound |
| Sibilance | A run of hissing sounds: s, sh, z and soft c | Several in a short span, not one or two |
| Onomatopoeia | A word whose sound imitates what it names | The word itself imitates. A line that merely "sounds harsh" is something else |
| Euphony and cacophony | A smooth run of sounds, or a clashing one | Say which sounds make it so |
| Refrain | A line or phrase that returns at intervals | Quote both places |
| Anaphora | The same word or phrase at the start of successive lines or clauses | At least two in a row |

## Rhyme

| Term | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| End rhyme | Rhyme between the last words of lines |
| Internal rhyme | Rhyme between a word inside a line and another in the same or the next line |
| Perfect rhyme | The last stressed vowel and every sound after it match, and the sound before that vowel differs |
| Near rhyme, slant rhyme, half rhyme | The sounds are close but not identical. Often the final consonants match and the vowels differ, or the reverse |
| Eye rhyme | Words spelled alike at the end but pronounced differently, such as "love" and "move" |
| Masculine rhyme | Rhyme on a single final stressed syllable |
| Feminine rhyme | Rhyme on a stressed syllable followed by an unstressed one |
| Rhyme scheme | The pattern of end rhymes, written with letters: ABAB, AABB |

To test a pair, call `pronounce_word` on each word and compare the `Rhyme sound` lines. Identical rhyme sounds mean a perfect rhyme in the dictionary's modern American pronunciation. A shared final consonant with different vowels is a near rhyme. A pair that fails today may have rhymed for the poet, so in an old poem say "in modern pronunciation" before calling a rhyme imperfect.

## Lines and sentences

| Term | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| End stopped line | The sense and the punctuation pause at the end of the line |
| Enjambment | The sentence runs over the line end without a pause |
| Caesura | A marked pause inside a line, usually at punctuation |
| Stanza | A group of lines set apart by space. Couplet has two lines, tercet three, quatrain four, sestet six, octave eight |
| Volta | The turn in thought in a sonnet, often at line nine or before the final couplet |
| Elision | A syllable dropped or slurred to fit the meter, sometimes shown by an apostrophe |

## Meter in brief

Meter is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables a line is built on. The full vocabulary, and how to scan with the pronunciation tool, is in the `rhyme-and-meter` skill at `../../rhyme-and-meter/references/meter-and-scansion.md`. In a reading, three points are usually enough:

1. Name the base pattern only if most lines keep it.
2. Point to a line that departs from it.
3. Say what the departure does at that moment.

## Diction

| Term | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Diction | The poet's choice of words: plain or ornate, concrete or abstract, native or Latinate |
| Register | The level of formality or the social setting a word belongs to |
| Denotation and connotation | What a word names, and what it suggests beyond that |
| Archaism | A word or form that was already old when the poet used it |

`define_word` separates senses of a word. It is a modern general dictionary with no dates or usage labels, so it cannot tell you whether a sense was current for the poet. Use it to lay out the possible senses, then argue from the line which one is in play.

## Habits that keep a reading honest

- Quote the words you are discussing.
- Count before you generalise. "Mostly monosyllables" should survive a count.
- One strong observation tied to its effect is worth more than a list of devices.
- Say "I read this as" for interpretation, and keep it apart from description.
- When evidence is mixed, say so.
